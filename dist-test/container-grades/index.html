Grades

Container grades explained

There are three grades worth talking about. The gap between them is usually wider than the gap between two suppliers quoting you on the same grade, which is why the grade is the first question we ask and the last thing you should compare on.

Every enquiry answered within one business day.You hear back from a person, not an autoresponder. Enquiries that land on a weekend are answered the next business day.

New (single-trip)

Built, loaded once, shipped once, unloaded here. Straight walls, clean floor, unfaded paint, seals that have never been out in the weather for long. If the container is going somewhere it will be looked at — a front yard, a shopfront, a display — this is the one.

Sold watertight: yes — checked wind and watertight before it leaves.

Cargo-worthy used

A working container that is still certified fit to carry freight at sea. Dents, surface rust and old shipping-line paint are normal and expected. Wind and watertight is checked before it leaves. This is the grade most people should buy and about seventy per cent of what we sell.

Sold watertight: yes — checked wind and watertight before it leaves.

As-is

Retired from sea service and sold on its faults. Cheapest by a distance and completely fine for a lot of jobs — a tool lock-up under cover, a bund, a site office shell, a base for a build. Not sold watertight. We photograph the faults and tell you what they are before you buy it.

Sold watertight: no. This grade is not sold watertight.

Grade matters more than anything else in a container quote. Cargo-worthy is the grade we check wind and watertight before it leaves the yard. As-is units are cheaper again and are not sold watertight — one may carry a patched repair, a soft spot in the floor, or a door seal that no longer seats properly, and we will tell you exactly what is wrong with a particular unit rather than let you find it out on delivery day. New single-trip containers are unmarked, sealed, and have made one loaded voyage. If the container has to keep rain off whatever goes inside it, buy cargo-worthy or better.

In practice

What actually separates two used containers

Stand two cargo-worthy 20fts side by side and the difference in what they are worth is almost entirely in two places: the floor and the doors. A solid 28mm marine-ply floor with no soft spots and doors that pull up hard against a seated gasket is a good container regardless of how the paint looks.

Dents in the side panels, surface rust and three layers of old shipping-line livery look far worse than they are. Cosmetics are the cheapest thing to live with and the easiest thing to be talked into paying for.

Common questions

About container grades

What does cargo-worthy mean?

That the container is still certified fit to carry freight at sea. Structurally sound, no holes, doors sealing properly, floor solid, and able to pass survey. It is a working standard rather than a cosmetic one, so dents, surface rust and old shipping-line paint are entirely normal on a cargo-worthy unit. Every cargo-worthy container we sell is checked wind and watertight before it leaves.

What does as-is mean?

Retired from sea service and sold on its faults. It may have a patched repair, a soft section of floor, a door seal that no longer seats or a hole somewhere. As-is units are not sold watertight. What we will always do is tell you what is actually wrong with the specific unit and photograph it, so you buy it knowing. For plenty of jobs it is the right and much cheaper answer.

What is a single-trip container?

A new container that has been built overseas, loaded once, shipped here and unloaded — so it has made exactly one voyage. Effectively new: straight walls, clean floor, unmarked paint, seals that have not spent years in the weather. Almost every container sold as new in Australia is a single-trip unit, because building them here does not happen at any scale.

Does grade affect the price more than size?

Usually, yes, and that surprises people. The gap between an as-is 20ft and a new single-trip 20ft is far wider than the gap between a used 20ft and a used 40ft. It is also the gap most likely to explain why one supplier's quote looks better than another's — check you are comparing the same grade before you compare the numbers.

Are all your containers watertight?

No, and any supplier who says all of theirs are is being loose with the word. Cargo-worthy and new units are checked wind and watertight before they leave. As-is units are explicitly not sold watertight — that is the whole reason they cost less. Which grade you need is decided by what is going inside.

Why this one

When a 10ft is the right call

  • Goes down the side of a suburban house where a 20ft never will. On a standard side passage that one fact is often the whole decision.
  • Lands off a plain tilt-tray and needs roughly half the run-in a 20ft does, so the delivery is cheaper and a great many more sites work.
  • Costs more per cubic metre than a 20ft every single time. If the room exists for the bigger unit, the bigger unit is nearly always the better buy — and we will say so on the phone rather than sell you the small one.

Worth knowing before you order

Ten-footers are the scarcest size in the country. Used stock moves through the yard in days rather than weeks, so a price quoted a month ago is not a price today. Ring and ask what is actually standing on the ground this week.

What fits

What fits in a 10 foot shipping container

Fifteen point nine cubic metres is easy to say and hard to picture, so here is the same volume described five different ways. Pick whichever one is closest to your job.

  • A ride-on mower, a wheelbarrow and a rack of long-handled tools, with room left to walk in and get at them without shuffling everything else out first.
  • About four standard Australian pallets on the floor in a single layer — the internal width takes two side by side and the length takes two deep. Stack them and you double it.
  • A compressor, a welder, a parts bench down one wall and shelving above it. A tidy one-man workshop, minus the room to swing anything long.
  • Roughly a single garage of household gear: forty to fifty removalist cartons stacked to head height, plus the awkward things — bikes, a fridge, a mattress on its edge.
  • A two-bedroom unit's furniture at a squeeze, packed properly, with nothing in it you would be upset to see scuffed.

Value

Is a 10ft container worth it, or should you buy a 20ft?

This is the question we get asked most often about the size, and the honest answer is arithmetic rather than opinion. Divide the starting price of a cargo-worthy unit by the volume you get for it and the ranking never changes.

If a 20ft will physically fit and a truck can get to the spot, the 20ft is the better buy nearly every time — well over twice the room for roughly half the money. That is not a sales line, it is the reason we talk a fair few people out of the 10ft on the phone. The 10ft earns its price in exactly one situation: when the bigger unit will not fit, or will not get in. A narrow side passage, a setback that leaves nothing spare, a courtyard with one gate. In that case the comparison is not a 10ft against a 20ft at all — it is a 10ft against no container.

Siting

Siting a 10ft storage container on a tight block

A 10ft weighs about 1,300 kg empty, so it does not need a slab. What it needs is four firm points. Timber sleepers, concrete blocks or a couple of pavers under each corner casting, levelled to within about 25mm end to end, with the ground under it draining away rather than holding water. Level matters more on a short container than people expect: the doors are the same size as a 20ft's and they are hung on the same frame, so a twist that a longer box would shrug off will stop a 10ft's doors lining up.

Two things go wrong more often than everything else combined. The first is setting the unit straight onto lawn, where the corners sink unevenly through a wet summer and the doors start to bind. The second is the door swing — each leaf opens out about 1.2 metres, so a unit backed hard against a fence or a wall is a unit you cannot open. Work out which way the doors face before delivery day, not after.

Ground clearance is worth a thought too. Sitting the container 100mm or so up on blocks keeps air under the floor, keeps the bearers out of standing water and makes it far easier to get a jack or a set of forks under it later.

Moving it later

Can a 10ft container be moved later?

Yes, and it is one of the quiet advantages of the size. A 10ft comes off a plain tilt-tray, so shifting it later is a straightforward job rather than a crane booking, and it will fit back out through the same gap it came in through.

Empty it before it is lifted. The frame carries the load through the four corner castings, not through the floor, and a container packed to the roof is being asked to do something it was not designed to do while it is on the tray. Check the doors close and latch first as well — if a unit has been sitting on soft ground for a few years the corners will have settled into it, and that is much easier to sort out before it moves than after. Grades

Which 10ft containers are sold watertight

Cargo-worthy is the grade we check wind and watertight before it leaves the yard, and a new single-trip unit has seals that have barely seen weather. Those are the two grades to buy if the container has to keep rain off whatever goes inside it. As-is 10ft units are cheaper again and are not sold watertight — they are sold on their faults, and we photograph those faults and tell you what they are rather than let you find them on delivery day. On a 10ft the grade question is sharper than on any other size, because the size is scarce and an as-is unit is often the only one on the ground.

Common questions

About 10ft containers

What are the dimensions of a 10ft shipping container?

Externally 2.99m × 2.44m × 2.59m, internally 2.83m × 2.35m × 2.39m. The door opening is 2.34m × 2.28m and the internal volume is 15.9m³. Tare weight is approx 1,300 kg. Those are standard ISO figures and they do not vary meaningfully between manufacturers — what does vary is the condition of the floor and the doors, which is a grade question rather than a size one.

What fits in a 10ft container?

A ride-on, a workbench and wall shelving. Roughly a single garage's worth of gear. Two-bedroom unit's furniture at a squeeze, if it is packed properly and nothing is precious.

How much does a 10ft shipping container cost?

Cargo-worthy used 10ft units start from $4,350 and new single-trip from $6,400, both guide prices ex GST. What moves them is condition, what is on the ground this week and which depot the unit has to come out of. Delivery is quoted separately with the container because it varies so much with distance and access.

What does a 10ft container need for delivery?

A 10ft lands off a plain tilt-tray and needs roughly half the run-in a 20ft does — call it 12 metres of straight approach, 3 metres at the narrowest point and 4.5 metres of headroom for the tray to lift. That is the whole reason the size exists: it makes tight suburban blocks, narrow side passages and awkward inner-city courtyards possible where nothing longer works.

Is a 10 foot shipping container worth it?

Only when the space decides it. Per cubic metre a 10ft is the dearest container we sell, by a wide margin — a cargo-worthy 20ft holds more than twice as much for roughly half the money. If the block will take the bigger unit, buy the bigger unit. If it will not, the 10ft is a genuinely good box and the only one that will fit.

Can a 10ft container be moved once it is on site?

Yes. It comes off a plain tilt-tray and goes back on one, so relocating it later is a normal job rather than a crane booking. Empty it first — the frame carries the load through the corner castings, not the floor — and check the doors still close and latch before anything lifts it.

What ground does a 10ft storage container need?

Four firm, level points rather than a slab. Sleepers, concrete blocks or pavers under the corner castings, levelled to within about 25mm end to end, on ground that drains away. Sitting it 100mm up on blocks keeps air under the floor. And leave about 1.2 metres in front of the doors for the leaves to swing.

Are 10ft containers watertight?

Cargo-worthy and new single-trip units are — cargo-worthy is checked wind and watertight before it leaves the yard. As-is 10ft units are not sold watertight. No container of any grade is flood protection: water that reaches the door seals gets in, and an empty container will float.
